# Legends of Gomsdu — Vibe Document

**Purpose of this doc:** This is not a mechanics spec. This is the feel — the thing every system should be checked against before it's considered "done." If a mechanic technically works but doesn't serve what's written here, it's not finished yet. This doc exists because a prior session understood the *ingredients* (serious subject matter, Seinfeld-style humor) without understanding how they *combine*. Read this first, every time, before touching Dialogue or tone-adjacent systems.

---

## The Core Premise: The Player Is Not From Gomsdu

Gomsdu's inhabitants sense something is off about the Player. In-world, this is explained by contact with Kulkrow's Shield — a clean, lore-legible alibi.

The real reason is structural, not narrative: **the Player is the only entity in Gomsdu experiencing it from outside its own internal logic.** They are, literally, not from this world. They're playing it from a 4th-wall-adjacent remove that no NPC shares.

This produces a permanent, built-in asymmetry:

- **To the World and its inhabitants:** nothing is odd, weird, disturbing, or backwards. Everything is simply how things are. Gomsdu's logic is self-consistent and NPCs never question it.
- **To the Player:** it should constantly feel a little twisted, a little off — not because the World is broken, but because the Player is bringing outside expectations to a place that owes them nothing.

**This asymmetry is the engine. It is not decoration on top of the lore — it is a source of mechanics in its own right**, the same way cosmology is a source of mechanics. When in doubt about whether something is "on vibe," check: does this lean on the gap between World-normal and Player-perspective? If a system could just as easily exist in a generic fantasy RPG with the names swapped, it's probably not using the gap, and it's probably not distinctly Gomsdu.

## The Tone Mechanism: Absurdity as Satire

This is **not** comic relief bolted onto serious material. It is **not** tonal whiplash (Discworld-style "funny things happen in a serious world"). It is not jokes written *into* the world to lighten it.

**The humor is the satire, delivered through the World's own dead-straight internal logic.**

Nothing in Gomsdu is played for a laugh on its own terms. The comedy comes entirely from the gap between what the World treats as unremarkable and what the Player's imported, outside perspective flags as absurd. This is the Seinfeld inspiration, properly understood: it's not that funny things happen — it's that ordinary things, taken completely seriously by everyone involved, become funny once you notice what they reveal.

**Practical implication:** Don't write jokes. Write a World that is fully, humorlessly consistent with itself — and trust the Player's outside vantage point to generate the comedy from that consistency. The moment the World starts winking at its own absurdity, the mechanism breaks. Gomsdu doesn't know it's funny. That's the whole point.

---

## Structural Instances (Not Just Narrative Ones)

The gap shouldn't live only in writing and dialogue — it should show up in *mechanics themselves* wherever possible. Example already built:

- **Combat vs. Exchange, time pressure.** Genre convention: combat is the tense, clock-driven, high-stakes system; conversation is the low-stakes space to think as long as you like. Gomsdu inverts this — Combat gives unlimited planning time on the Sequencer; Exchange puts a hard countdown on the Player the instant someone Calls. This is absurd by outside genre expectations and completely unremarkable within Gomsdu's own logic. That's the 4th-wall gap, made mechanical instead of only narrative.

When designing new systems, actively look for places this inversion can be built into rules, not just flavor text. A mechanic that violates the Player's genre instincts while staying perfectly natural in-world is doing double duty — it's both good design and on-vibe satire at the same time.

## TATA: The Unknowable Hand

Duality isn't one theme among several in Gomsdu — it's the theme everything else sits on top of. Gom and Kulkrow's cosmological mirroring is one expression of it. **TATA is the same principle at the table.**

TATA is a single entity, not two roles taken in turns. Architect and Adversary operate *simultaneously*, inside the same actor, with no seam between them. There's no moment where TATA switches from building to opposing — both are always true at once, and which one is driving any given decision is never something the Player can cleanly pull apart. "Tata for now" names the person stepping into that combined role — not into half of it.

The words are doing real work. *Architect* — design, structure, intention, something built with care. *Adversary* — opposition, obstruction, the one who tests. Fusing both into one unbroken actor means the Player can never trust that a piece of the World is *only* an opportunity or *only* a threat, or know which hand actually shaped it.

**The mechanic this produces:** Fog of War in Gomsdu isn't just hidden stats or unseen enemies — it's asymmetry of *authorship*. The Player experiences outcomes without ever learning whether Architect or Adversary was behind them. Only TATA — and, notionally, the World itself — knows. This gives the Information pillar a concept it was missing: not just *what* a Character knows (content-asymmetry), but *why* they know it and whose hand gave or withheld it (authorship-asymmetry). Carry this into the Information Panel work in the mechanics doc — it's the viable version of that system.

**TATA Veil Vision** is this made literal at the interface level: a distinct vision layer the Player can't see behind, showing different information depending on who currently holds it.

**The table is [Player, World, TATA] — always, not just when a human's in the TATA seat.** AI TATA isn't a fallback mode for when nobody wants the job; it's the default configuration the rest of the game is built on top of. Every System — Combat, Exchange, whatever comes after — has to account for all three parties whether or not a human ever sits in TATA. This is why solo play has to work start to finish: one human Player, World, and TATA (AI-run) is a complete table, not a stripped-down one.

**By default, TATA stays hidden — Players don't see the seam at all.** Most tables should never be shown that the role exists, let alone who's behind it; that's the "unknowable hand" held completely intact, not just narratively but at the interface level. Revealing it is a deliberate grant from whoever's hosting — never a joining Player's own call, and never something stumbled into during onboarding.

## Design Priority: Dialogue Is the Flagship

Combat should land as **neat**.
Dialogue should land as **impressive**.

This isn't a value judgment on Combat — it's a resourcing and ambition call. When a tradeoff arises between polishing Combat further versus deepening Dialogue, Dialogue wins by default. The Dialogue Engine is where this project's central idea (mechanics derived from the World's own logic, not genre convention) gets its clearest, highest-stakes expression, because dialogue is where genre convention is strongest and most invisible everywhere else.

---

## Guiding Principle: Shared Grammar, Not Bespoke Systems

The failure mode from the last major design session: over-engineering every individual potential case instead of building a small set of primitives that naturally recombine.

The fix, going forward: build **a few core mechanisms that repeat across contexts**, rather than a special system per situation. Evidence this is already working:

- The **temporary → resolved** pattern (Flow/Focus in Combat; temporary Relationship → final Relationship in an Exchange) is one mechanism doing two jobs. Don't invent a second version of it. Reach for the existing one first.
- **Local vs. global** (immediate/lingering) is one axis, applied uniformly across Information, Personality, and Relationship. Learning it once teaches it three times.
- **Prepared vs. live** (Catch Phrases in Combat-adjacent prep; Conscious/Semi-Conscious/Subconscious Lines in Dialogue) is one tension, generalized.

Before building anything new: check whether an existing pattern already covers it in disguise. If it does, reuse it. If a genuinely new primitive is needed, keep it small enough that it could plausibly get reused somewhere else later — don't build something that only ever serves one Set Piece or one scenario.

## Quick Self-Check for New Material

Before calling a Dialogue (or any tone-adjacent) system finished, ask:

1. Does this lean on the Player/World perspective gap, or could it exist in any generic RPG?
2. Is the World playing this straight, or is it winking? (It should always be playing it straight.)
3. Does this reuse an existing primitive, or did it just invent a bespoke one-off? *(Engineering check.)*
4. Does this move the table through Surprise → Realization → Wonder — not just correctness? *(Emotional check. Realization is the hinge: it's what turns a surprising moment into one worth feeling awe about, rather than just noise.)*
5. Would this actually land, once real friends sit down and play it? *(Playtest check — the only one that isn't a guess.)*
